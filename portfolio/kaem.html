What are the requirements for discovering the true, agreed-upon, standard representations across images, language, and scientific data?

  1. standardised methodologies must corroborate, not automated tuning or guessing
  2. sustainable the scientific method is iterative - repeated across RNGs, datasets, optimisation dynamics, scales, architectures
  3. interpretable predictions at the edge of human knowledge must be verifiable and understood
  4. informed centuries worth of knowledge shouldn't be discarded, and we can't rely entirely on data
  5. accessible knowledgeable and passionate people from many different backgrounds will use AI

KAEM: Kolmogorov-Arnold Energy Model

Paper → JAX package → Julia repository →

KAEM is an unsupervised generative model built for the above requirements.

Like a VAE, it draws a low-dimensional latent sample and decodes it into data. Unlike a VAE, the latent prior isn't a fixed Gaussian. It's a set of learned 1D densities based on Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks ( arXiv 2404.19756 ). They can be interpreted and sampled in a fast and exact single forward pass by inverse transform, no iterative denoising.

The papers also introduces annealing as a highly parallel, interpretable, speed-preserving alternative to diffusion modelling.

Standardised

The Kolmogorov-Arnold Representation Theorem decomposes any continuous multivariate function into two finite sums of univariate ones:

  • Deterministic
  • Definitive
  • Universal for continuous functions

This isn't readily applied to probabilistic modelling. KAEM gets there by recasting the inner functions \(\psi_{q,p} : [0,1] \to \mathbb{R}\) as Markov kernels, inverse CDFs acting on uniform noise.

Inverse transform sampling becomes a measurable map from uniform noise into the target, so the KA therorem's determinism is preserved and a probabilistic interpretation falls out without introducing stochasticity into the representation.

The result is one standard skeleton across modalities.

Sustainable

Wu et al. (2022) report a 30 : 29 : 41 lifecycle split for an industrial-scale language model, across data processing, experimentation/training, and inference ( arXiv 2111.00364 ).

Inference is the largest slice, so KAEM keeps it fast (single forward pass). Annealing only runs during training, and parallelises across temperatures instead of paying a sequential cost every generation like diffusion. Experimentation is also large. A standardised representation with stronger inductive bias reduces the architecture search/tuning and reliance on guessing.

Sampling speed comparable to VAE, ~700x faster than DDPM

Interpretable

The prior is a product of 1D densities, since the Kolmogorov-Arnold Theorem provides the basis. You can plot/interpret them, modes and tails are easily visible. Contrastive divergence, (the criterion used to train EBMs, aligning prior to posterior via empirical Bayes), sculpts them to data. Interpolate between two latent samples, and the traversal has meaning you can read off the plot.

A straight line between two latent samples would cut through the empty centre of the space and decode to something illegible. SLERP traverses the arc where the samples actually live, so every step stays a plausible sample.

Informed

Three places to inject what you know about the problem:

  • the reference prior
  • the generator architecture
  • the choice of univariate basis functions

Pick the ones that match the domain. KAEM's architecture enables this.

Accessible

KAEM is a robust probabilistic model. It can run on laptops.

With the correct priors and biases, posterior expectations can be estimated by importance sampling, (a simple weighted average over samples). No large encoder network needed, just a sample-based estimator with no parameters of its own. And generation is a fast and exact inverse transform on uniform noise, no iteration at inference.

The univariate 1D priors are also easy to plot and understand. A domain expert without a computational statistics background can read them.

Training time per step. KAEM at 4.6 ms beats VAE at 5.8 ms across latent dims.

Metrics

How do we quantify the perceptual quality of generated images?

It's not exact, but the academic community has adopted the following approach. Pass real and generated images through InceptionV3, then measure distance between the two datasets in InceptionV3's feature space.

Two standard ways to compute that distance:

FID Fréchet Inception Distance ( arXiv 1706.08500 )

Models the two feature distributions as Gaussians, then finds the Fréchet distance.

KID Kernel Inception Distance ( arXiv 1801.01401 )

Kernel-based, drops the Gaussian assumption.

However, FID and KID are biased in two ways:

  1. InceptionV3 is trained on ImageNet, so scores favour ImageNet-like distributions.
  2. FID N is finite-sample biased: \(\mathbb{E}[\text{FID}_N] \neq \text{FID}_\infty\) , so fixed N is unreliable.

My experimetns used effectively unbiased estimators by applying linear regression across sample sizes {2000, 4000, ..., 20,000} to extrapolate to N=∞ ( arXiv 1911.07023 ). This gives FID ∞ and KID ∞ - unbiased estimators of FID/KID at infinite samples.

Compilation

Julia ↗ | JAX ↗

The paper's experiments were conducted in Julia, since it needs more love and its primary weakness is adoption! The JAX implementation has been provided as a more polished, production-level package.

Julia native performance, no Python/C++ split
Lux.jl pure functions -> full visibility to compiler optimisations, (same as Flax)
Reactant.jl compiles Julia to XLA/MLIR, an open standard that any hardware can target
Enzyme.jl autodiff at MLIR level, after multiple levels of optimisations

The compiler stack underneath:

MLIR Multi-Level Intermediate Representation ( arXiv 2002.11054 )

Open and modular compiler IR. Keeps IR high-level (immutable SSA tensors) for as long as possible so linear-algebra + fusion optimisations work on the maths itself. Only bufferises to mutable 'memrefs' when it's absolutely needed for memory planning and (pseudo-polyhedral) transformations. Importantly, MLIR is retargettable across hardware as compute vendors can simply plug in at the lowest level and reuse the other levels/optimisations that they need.

XLA Accelerated Linear Algebra ( openxla.org )

Google's linear-algebra compiler, providing op fusion, memory-layout optimisation, multi-device scheduling. Targets CPU, GPU, TPU via MLIR.

Reactant traces the entire training step (model, loss, sampler, optimiser) into one StableHLO program. XLA then runs whole-program optimisation:

  • kernel fusion - small ops collapse into fewer and larger kernels, eliminating memory round-trips
  • dead-code and common-subexpression elimination (CSE) across the whole program
  • memory-layout optimisation matched to the compiled access pattern

PyTorch differentiates eagerly. JAX differentiates the trace before XLA sees it.

Enzyme differentiates after multiple optimisation passes, so the backward pass inherits every optimisation the forward pass earned.

This is the biggest draw for me, Enzyme is the fastest AD library. It's also being worked on for JAX (Enzyme-JAX) , but it's not quite ready for deep learning.

How KAEM benefits:

  1. the Langevin sampling chain is compiled as one kernel; the iterated 'state' (data and latent samples, noise, annealed temperatures, etc.) stays in fast memory across every step
  2. the score \(\nabla \log \pi\) is found by Enzyme AD at each Langevin step, inside the same kernel
  3. \(\partial \mathcal{L} / \partial \theta\) by Enzyme on the outer step, with MCMC samples detached as constants
  4. the same compiled program retargets to any MLIR-backed accelerator

Closing

Generative models are often built bottom-up. Create and scale an architecture, tune until the metrics improve. The design principles get interpreted afterwards from whatever happened to work.

KAEM was built differently. There is still no established, agreed-upon, standard representation for language, images, and intelligence. So KAEM started from the 5 specs defined at the top (based on my rudimentary understanding of the scientific method) and was engineered to meet them. And it's the closest I could have come to a model that meets all 5 specs at once, with:

  • a deterministic KA theorem skeleton
  • a prior you can plot
  • single-pass inference
  • room for domain knowledge

The specs defined the model, not the other way round.

In reality, it doesn't yet reach all 5. It's only been tested on small, (yet standard and consistent with the literature) image datasets at my limited budget, not the language or scientific data I'm ultimately aiming at. Plus optimiser dynamics are such that different initialisations could produce different representations, so KAEM isn't yet the reproducible standard the first spec demands. Overall, there's major work to do beyond this introductory paper.

But I still believe it's a big step towards my 5 requirements, and a broader, grander, more ambitious conceptualisation:

"The Kolmogorov-Arnold theorem is all you need."
