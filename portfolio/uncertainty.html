Reasoning under uncertainty

A self-driving car that's 99% accurate gets 1 in 100 decisions wrong. That decision could happen at a school crossing.

Uncertainty quantification is how engineers catch that last 1%.

Drug solubility on AqSolDB

AqSolDB is a dataset of experimental solubility values. Solubility is a great predictor of whether a drug candidate makes it through trials, since it controls:

  • bioavailability - poor solubility reduces oral absorption into the bloodstream
  • formulation - which excipients and delivery methods are viable
  • ADMET profile - absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity
  • clinical attrition - solubility problems can be a leading cause of late-stage failure

Solubility predictions are full of uncertainty:

Aleatoric irreducible noise in measurements

Two identical experiments might give different solubilities. More data shrinks the standard error of the mean, but can't remove the noise itself.

Epistemic reducible lack of knowledge

What the dataset doesn't reflect because nothing nearby has been measured. Can be reduced by taking measurements around or expanding the training set to unseen regions of the problem domain.

Aleatoric sources

  • conformational flexibility - molecules rotate around their single bonds and sample many 3D shapes. Each measurement samples a random conformer from a thermodynamic ensemble - many possibilities. The relative weights come from a Boltzmann distribution over force-field energies. ETKDG ( RDKit cookbook ) generates plausible samples, and the Merck Molecular Force Field ( MMFF wiki ) ranks them by minimised energy.
Conformers of a flexible molecule. Lowest-energy state under MMFF minimisation vs a higher-energy state
  • aggregation states - above the critical micelle concentration (CMC) , amphiphilic drug molecules (with both hydrophobic and hydrophilic regions) cluster into micelles instead of staying as monomers. The hydrophobic effect ( wiki page ) drives it. Water's hydrogen bonds are too strong to overcome, so instead, each molecule's hydrophobic regions get pulled inwards to minimise the hydrophobic surface area exposed to water. Meanwhile the hydrophilic regions face outwards and surround them. The apparent solubility depends on which state dominates. Clustering can lower it even when the molecular properties suggest higher solubility.
Monomers cluster into micelles above the CMC, driven by the hydrophobic effect
  • solvent and pH - hydrogen-bonding, ionisation state, temperature, all shift where the equilibrium lies
  • kinetic vs thermodynamic solubility - whether the measurement caught a metastable kinetic state or the true thermodynamic minimum

Epistemic sources

  • data sparsity - molecular property space is large, AqSolDB covers a thin portion. Anything outside is unseen
  • model choice - kernel encodes assumptions (smoothness, scale). If wrong, the model is biased!
  • inducing-point selection - sparse approximations replace the full posterior with one supported on a smaller set of representative points. Picked badly and much of the space remains unseen

Gaussian processes

This blog is great for understanding Gaussian Processes (GPs). Briefly, a GP is a distribution over functions, specified by a mean function \(m(x)\) and a covariance kernel \(k(x, x')\). For any finite set of inputs the function values are jointly Gaussian:

\[ \bigl(f(x_1), \ldots, f(x_n)\bigr) \;\sim\; \mathcal{N}\!\bigl(\mathbf{m},\, K\bigr), \qquad K_{ij} = k(x_i, x_j) \]

The kernel encodes what functions the GP considers plausible (smoothness, amplitude, length scale). It has to produce a valid covariance. The Gram matrix \(K\) must be positive semi-definite , meaning \(\mathbf{c}^\top K\, \mathbf{c} \geq 0\) for any vector \(\mathbf{c}\) (variance is non-negative in every direction).

Regression

With \(n\) observations \(y_i = f(x_i) + \varepsilon_i\) where \(\varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \lambda)\) (the aleatoric noise), predict \(f\) at new inputs \(X_*\). Gaussian prior + Gaussian noise -> the joint is also Gaussian:

\[ \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{y} \\ \mathbf{f}_* \end{pmatrix} \;\sim\; \mathcal{N}\!\left(\mathbf{0},\; \begin{pmatrix} K + \lambda I & K_{n*} \\ K_{*n} & K_{**} \end{pmatrix}\right) \]

The aleatoric noise lives on the diagonal and \(K + \lambda I\) is equivalently a noise-augmented kernel \[k_{\text{obs}}(x, y) = k(x, y) + \lambda\, \delta(x,y), \] where \(\delta\) is 1 when \(x = y\) and 0 otherwise. The kernel models the smooth signal, \(\lambda\) captures the aleatoric uncertainty, and off-diagonal covariances stay simple so the GP can interpolate smoothly between data points.

Conditioning \(\mathbf{f}_*\) on \(\mathbf{y}\) gives the posterior predictive :

\[ \mathbf{f}_* \mid \mathbf{y} \;\sim\; \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_*,\, \Sigma_*), \] \[ \boldsymbol{\mu}_* = K_{*n}(K + \lambda I)^{-1}\mathbf{y}, \] \[ \Sigma_* = K_{**} - K_{*n}(K + \lambda I)^{-1}K_{n*}. \]

This form naturally supports UQ in two ways:

  • \(\boldsymbol{\mu}_*\) is the prediction, \(\mathrm{diag}(\Sigma_*)\) is the epistemic uncertainty at each test point. "Predictive variance" for a new observation means adding the aleatoric term back: \(\mathrm{Var}(y_*) = \Sigma_* + \lambda I\).
  • \(\Sigma_*\) depends only on where the training inputs are. Confidence is dependent on location, not the magnitude/values of data. Cover the input space and uncertainty falls regardless of what values \(\mathbf{y}\) adopts.

Marginal likelihood

The kernel has free parameters \(\theta\) (length scales \(\sigma_i\), noise \(\lambda\)), chosen by maximising the marginal likelihood , the probability of \(\mathbf{y}\) with all latent functions marginalised out:

\[ \log p(\mathbf{y} \mid \theta) \;=\; \underbrace{-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{y}^\top(K + \lambda I)^{-1}\mathbf{y}}_{\text{data fit}} \;\underbrace{-\,\tfrac{1}{2}\log\bigl|K + \lambda I\bigr|}_{\text{complexity}} \;-\; \tfrac{n}{2}\log 2\pi. \]

A flexible model (short length scale) makes the data-fit term small but inflates the eigenvalues of \(K\) and incurs a complexity penalty. The max solution balances them and Occam's razor falls out as a side effect. The data picks the simplest adequate model. GPs are implicitly regularised.

Fit the hyperparameters this way and you get the posterior uncertainty surface on each sample:

Posterior standard deviation on AqSolDB. The histogram is bimodal with a small in-distribution mode at ~0.4 and a much larger extrapolative mode at ~0.85, so most of feature space is somewhere the model knows it doesn't know.

Kernels

The Kernel Cookbook provides a list for picking and composing Kernels, which determine what features/variances to target.

Every kernel comes with its own reproducing-kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) \(\mathcal{H}_k\): the space of functions it can represent. Smoother kernels assign a bigger norm to functions that vary wildly, so during marginal-likelihood fitting the GP pays a complexity penalty for fitting something its kernel can't naturally express. Meaning kernels provide natural inductive bias to guide learning:

RBF squared exponential

\(k(x, y) = \exp(-\|x - y\|_\Sigma^2 / 2)\). Sample paths are infinitely differentiable, the RKHS contains only entire functions. Spectral density is itself a Gaussian, so high frequencies decay exponentially. Great when fitting smooth, unsuitable when nonsmooth.

Matérn tunable smoothness

\(k\) is a function of \(\|x-y\|\) and a smoothness parameter \(\nu\). Sample paths are \(\lceil\nu\rceil - 1\) times differentiable. \(\nu = 1/2\) gives continuous but nowhere differentiable paths (the exponential kernel), \(\nu \to \infty\) recovers RBF. Lower \(\nu\) handles sharper features.

RQ rational quadratic, scale mixture

\(k(x, y) = (1 + \|x-y\|_\Sigma^2 / (2\alpha))^{-\alpha}\). The marginal of a Gamma-mixture over RBF length scales. Handles functions with both local and global structure. \(\alpha \to \infty\) recovers RBF, \(\alpha \to 0\) gives heavier tails.

Per-dimension length scales \(\sigma_i\) give automatic relevance determination : dimensions that don't affect \(f\) drift to large \(\sigma_i\) at the marginal-likelihood optimum, effectively dropping out.

Sparse GPs: \(\mathcal{O}(n^3)\) is the problem

The dense posterior needs \((K + \lambda I)^{-1}\), which involves \(\mathcal{O}(n^3)\) time complexity and \(\mathcal{O}(n^2)\) storage for the matrix inversion. AqSolDB has ~10,000 molecules! Sparse GPs instead subsample \(m \ll n\) inducing points \(Z\) and approximate \(K\) by a low-rank structure using those points.

Fully Independent Training Conditional (FITC, arXiv 1606.04820 ) replaces \(K + \lambda I\) with

\[ K_{\mathrm{FITC}} \;=\; \underbrace{K_{nm}K_{mm}^{-1}K_{mn}}_{\text{low-rank}} \;+\; \underbrace{\Lambda}_{\text{diagonal correction}}\]

\[ \Lambda \;=\; \mathrm{diag}\!\bigl(K - K_{nm}K_{mm}^{-1}K_{mn}\bigr) + \lambda I \]

The diagonal \(\Lambda\) restores the correct marginal variance at each training point, folding the approximation error into the per-point noise term. Because \(K_{\mathrm{FITC}}\) is low-rank plus diagonal, the Woodbury inversion identity reduces the inversion to an \(m \times m\) solve, bringing cost to \(\mathcal{O}(nm^2 + m^3)\).

  • dense GP - \(\mathcal{O}(n^3)\) compute, \(\mathcal{O}(n^2)\) storage, exact posterior variance
  • FITC - \(\mathcal{O}(nm^2 + m^3)\) compute, \(\mathcal{O}(nm)\) storage, exact diagonal variance and approximate off-diagonal

Active Learning

github ↗

Wet-lab solubility measurements are expensive. Each trial burns hours to days of work, milligrams of compound, spectrometer runs, etc. The space of drug-like molecules runs into \(10^{60}\), AqSolDB has labelled around \(10^4\).

The posterior variance \(\Sigma_*\) at each candidate point says how much labelling it would reduce the model's epistemic uncertainty. Regions of high uncertainty guide where you'll next prioritise experimentation, synthesis, or risk assessments.

It's not enough to simply make decisions. You have to quantify the validity of them.
