KAEM: Kolmogorov-Arnold Energy Model

Generative models succumb to trade-offs. GANs sacrifice training stability, VAEs sample quality, diffusion inference speed. KAEM addresses all three.

Read the paper →

KAEM is a latent-variable generative model! Like a VAE, it draws a low-dimensional latent sample and decodes it into data. Unlike a VAE, the latent prior isn't a fixed Gaussian. It's a set of learned 1D densities based on Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks ( arXiv 2404.19756 ). They can be interpreted and sampled in a fast and exact single forward pass by inverse transform, no iterative denoising.

Also read about how I introduce annealing as a highly parallel and interpretable alternative to diffusion modelling for KAEM's training!

What are the requirements for discovering the true, agreed-upon, standard representations across images, language, and scientific data?

  1. standardised methodologies must corroborate, no guessing
  2. sustainable the scientific method is iterative - repeated across RNGs, datasets, optimisation dynamics, scales, architectures
  3. interpretable predictions at the edge of human knowledge must be verifiable and understood
  4. informed centuries worth of human knowledge shouldn't be discarded, plus STEM data is from sensors, lab setups, and physical surrogates
  5. accessible knowledgeable and passionate people from many different backgrounds will use AI

Standardised

The Kolmogorov-Arnold Representation Theorem decomposes any continuous multivariate function into two finite sums of univariate ones. Deterministic. Definitive. Universal for continuous functions.

That isn't readily applied to probabilistic modelling. KAEM gets there by recasting the inner functions \(\psi_{q,p} : [0,1] \to \mathbb{R}\) as Markov kernels, inverse CDFs acting on uniform noise on [0,1].

Inverse transform sampling becomes a measurable map from uniform noise into the target, so KART's deterministic structure is preserved and a probabilistic interpretation falls out without introducing stochasticity into the representation.

The result is one skeleton across modalities. This is a new application of deterministic representation to generative modelling.

Disclosure: KART was strictly adhered to on simple datasets (MNIST, FMNIST), where a KAN generator with independent univariate priors worked well. On SVHN, CIFAR10, and CelebA, I cheated and used a CNN decoder for spatial inductive bias and a mixture-of-univariate prior instead of the strict factorised form. The deterministic skeleton works, but the RGB images lean on CNN bias too, not KART alone ( see the paper ).

Sustainable

Wu et al. (2022) report a 30 : 29 : 41 lifecycle split for an industrial-scale language model, across data processing, experimentation/training, and inference ( arXiv 2111.00364 ).

Inference is the largest slice, so KAEM keeps it fast (single forward pass). Annealing only runs during training, and parallelises across temperatures instead of paying a sequential cost every generation like diffusion. Experimentation is also large. A standardised representation with stronger inductive bias reduces the architecture search/tuning and reliance on guessing.

Sampling speed comparable to VAE, ~700x faster than DDPM

Interpretable

The prior is a product of 1D densities, since the Kolmogorov-Arnold Representation Theorem provides the basis. You can plot and interpret them, modes and tails are easily visible. Contrastive divergence (the criterion used to train EBMs, aligning prior to posterior like empirical Bayes) sculpts them to the data. Interpolate between two latent samples with SLERP, spherical linear interpolation (see image), and the traversal has a meaning you can read straight off the plot.

A straight line between two latent samples would cut through the empty centre of the space and decode to something illegible. SLERP traverses the arc where the samples actually live, so every step stays a plausible sample.

Informed

Three places to inject what you know about the problem:

  • the reference prior
  • the generator architecture
  • the choice of univariate basis functions

Pick the ones that match the domain. KAEM's architecture enables this.

Accessible

KAEM is a robust probabilistic model. It can run on laptops.

With the correct priors and biases, posterior expectations can be estimated by importance sampling (a simple weighted average over samples). No large encoder network needed, (unlike a VAE). Just a sample-based estimator with no parameters of its own. And generation is a fast and exact inverse transform on uniform noise, no iteration at inference.

The univariate 1D priors are easy to plot and understand. A domain expert without a computational statistics background can read them.

Training time per step. KAEM at 4.6 ms beats VAE at 5.8 ms across latent dims.

Metrics

How do we quantify the perceptual quality of generated images?

It's not exact, but the academic community has adopted the following approach. Pass real and generated images through InceptionV3, then measure distance between the two datasets in InceptionV3's feature space. Two standard ways to compute that distance.

FID Fréchet Inception Distance ( arXiv 1706.08500 )

Models the two feature distributions as Gaussians, then finds the Fréchet distance.

KID Kernel Inception Distance ( arXiv 1801.01401 )

Kernel-based, drops the Gaussian assumption.

However, FID and KID are biased in two ways:

  1. InceptionV3 is trained on ImageNet, so scores favour ImageNet-like distributions.
  2. FID N is finite-sample biased: \(\mathbb{E}[\text{FID}_N] \neq \text{FID}_\infty\) , so fixed N is unreliable.

We use effectively unbiased estimators by applying linear regression across sample sizes {2000, 4000, ..., 20,000} to extrapolate to N=∞ ( arXiv 1911.07023 ). This gives FID ∞ and KID ∞ , which are unbiased estimators of FID/KID at infinite samples.

Implementation

github ↗
Julia native performance, no Python/C++ split
Lux.jl pure functions -> full visibility to compiler optimisations
Reactant.jl compiles Julia to MLIR, an open standard any hardware can target
Enzyme.jl autodiff at MLIR level, after multiple levels of optimisations

The compiler stack underneath:

MLIR Multi-Level Intermediate Representation ( arXiv 2002.11054 )

Open and modular compiler IR. Keeps IR high-level (immutable SSA tensors) for as long as possible so linear-algebra + fusion optimisations work on the maths itself. Only bufferises to mutable memrefs when needed for memory planning and affine-dialect's (pseudo-polyhedral) transformations before lowering to hardware. MLIR is retargettable across hardware as vendors can plug in at the lowest level and reuse the other levels that they need.

XLA Accelerated Linear Algebra ( openxla.org )

Google's linear-algebra compiler, providing op fusion, memory-layout optimisation, multi-device scheduling. Targets CPU, GPU, TPU via MLIR.

Reactant traces the entire training step (model, loss, sampler, optimiser) into one StableHLO program. XLA then runs whole-program optimisation:

  • kernel fusion - small ops collapse into fewer and larger kernels, eliminating memory round-trips
  • dead-code and common-subexpression elimination (CSE) across the whole program
  • memory-layout optimisation matched to the compiled access pattern

PyTorch differentiates eagerly. JAX differentiates the trace before XLA optimises it. Enzyme differentiates after the optimisation passes, so the backward pass inherits every optimisation the forward pass earned.

How KAEM benefits:

  1. the Langevin sampling chain is compiled as one kernel; the iterated 'state' (data and latent samples, noise, annealed temperatures, etc.) stays in fast memory across every step
  2. the score \(\nabla \log \pi\) is found by Enzyme autodiff at each Langevin step, inside the same kernel
  3. \(\partial \mathcal{L} / \partial \theta\) by Enzyme on the outer step, with MCMC samples detached as constants
  4. the same compiled program retargets to any MLIR-backed accelerator

In PyTorch, each Langevin step would be interpreter overhead. In JAX, the sampler becomes a lax.scan with pure-functional state. No mutation and requires manual stop_gradient to detach MCMC samples from \(\partial \mathcal{L} / \partial \theta\) .

Reactant traces and optimises ordinary imperative Julia directly into MLIR. No special rewriting.

KAEM is as fast as the maths allows, on any hardware that MLIR targets.

Closing

Generative models are often built bottom-up. Create and scale an architecture, tune until the metrics improve. The design principles get interpreted afterwards from whatever happened to work.

KAEM was built differently. There is still no established, agreed-upon, standard representation for language, images, and intelligence. So KAEM started from the 5 specs defined at the top (based on my rudimentary understanding of the scientific method) and was engineered to meet them. And it's the closest I could have come to a model that meets all 5 specs at once, with:

  • a deterministic KART skeleton
  • a prior you can plot
  • single-pass inference
  • room for domain knowledge

The specs defined the model, not the other way round.

In reality, it doesn't yet reach all 5. It's only been tested on fairly small (yet standard and consistent with the literature) image datasets at my limited budget, not the language or scientific data I'm ultimately aiming at. Plus optimiser dynamics are such that different initialisations could produce different representations, so KAEM isn't yet the reproducible standard the first spec demands. Overall, there's major work to do beyond this introductory paper.

But I still believe it's a big step towards my 5 requirements, and a broader, grander, harder conceptualisation:

"The Kolmogorov-Arnold theorem is all you need."
