An intro to scientific machine learning

On an axes of big worldly problems, we have two resources at our disposal to model said problems: acquired human knowledge, and acquired data!

top-left

\(F = ma\) predicts trajectories from initial conditions. Maxwell's equations are closed-form. Newtonian mechanics gets you to the Moon and back!

low-right

Pretrain on the internet, LLMs pick up natural language without anyone writing down the dynamics of grammar. In recent history, this has been the point of deep learning as an endeavour.

middle

The vast majority of scientific phenomena lives somewhere in the middle. Partial knowledge, partial data, (obtained via lab setups, physical surrogates, sensors, expensive sims, etc.). Embedding that partial knowledge into a neural network is scientific machine learning ( arXiv 1711.10561 ).

And even when you have an abundance of data, (e.g. in something like the climate sciences), the complexity of the underlying manifold governing scientific phenomena is such that relationships aren't always discoverable from data-driven methods alone ( arXiv 2511.08860 ).

Neural Operators

Neural networks map between fixed discretisations, \(\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m\). Neural operators ( arXiv 2108.08481 ) map between infinite-dimensional function spaces : \[\mathcal{A}(D; \mathbb{R}^{d_a}) \to \mathcal{U}(D; \mathbb{R}^{d_u})\] Discretisation, \(D\), is a lossy sampling detail of the input function, (or equivalence class of functions). And trained at one resolution, neural operators can still generalise to others, (this is called zero-shot super-resolution, and it's not something attention can do).

This is awesome given that the natural world is field-valued with multiple degrees of freedom at each point in space-time, (the domain of partial differential equations, PDEs). If you apply a time-varying strain \(\epsilon(x, t)\) to a viscoelastic composite, it won't respond with a pointwise stress value or vector. It responds with a stress field \(\sigma(x, t)\).

Caveat: neural operators don't yet match the fidelity of numerical solvers (FEM, FVM, spectral methods, etc.) on most benchmarks. They trade accuracy for speed and discretisation invariance.

Integral Kernel Operator

A neural-operator layer has the form

\[ v_{t+1}(x) = \sigma\!\left(W v_t(x) + (\mathcal{K} v_t)(x) + b\right) \]

where the integral kernel operator (IKO) is

\[ (\mathcal{K} v_t)(x) = \int_D \kappa_\theta(x, y)\, v_t(y)\, dy. \]

The IKO is the only nonlocal piece of the layer, capturing how different points in space-time relate to one another. Every architecture in the family is a choice of how to parametrise \(\kappa_\theta\) . For example:

CNN translation-equivariant, compactly supported, weights shared across \(x\)

Kernel \(\kappa_\theta(x, y) = \kappa_\theta(x - y)\) with support on a small neighbourhood. Strong bias: locality and shift equivariance. \(O(k^d)\) parameters for a stencil of size \(k\) in \(d\) dimensions. But fails when the true kernel is global or anisotropic.

FNO Fourier Neural Operator ( arXiv 2010.08895 )

Same translation-invariant kernel \(\kappa(x - y)\) , global support. The convolution theorem turns the integral into a pointwise multiplication in the spectral domain, (spectral convolution), \((\mathcal{K} v)(x) = \mathcal{F}^{-1}\!\left(R_\theta \cdot \mathcal{F}(v)\right)(x)\) , with learnable weights \(R_\theta\) on a finite set of modes. Bias: dynamics live in a low-frequency band, (holds for many smooth PDEs).

Attn entirely data-dependent, learned per pair, no sharing ( arXiv 2105.14995 )

Kernel \(\kappa(x, y; v) = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(q(v(x)) \cdot k(v(y)) / \sqrt{d_k}\right)\) is itself a function of the input through learned projections \(q, k, V\) . All weights are learned from data at a specific discretisation, and they're not shared. It's uninformed about the problem and relies heavily on observational biases.

A bit of personal speculation

Transformers and attention have sequence length dimension (temporal) and an embedding dimension (spatial). Almost like a discretised 2D field.

And self-attention can be viewed as a specific application of the IKO at a specific discretisation, \((\mathcal{K} v)(x) = \int \kappa(x, y)\, v(y)\, dy\) , applied along the sequence axis. So the same operator learning maths applies to natural language and Transformers as it does below with viscoelastic materials, Darcy flows, and other PDE systems.

Which raises a question that needs asking: if the same framework fits viscoplastic mechanics, fluid flow, and natural language, is language itself a (very complicated) system of PDEs?

I strongly suspect yes, and there is likely a better representation for natural language somewhere out there in the void. Until we have it, attention is the best general-purpose substitute.

Where Attention fails

If you know the family of PDEs your data comes from, a model with the correct inductive biases outperforms on:

  • parameter and data efficiency - less storage and training required
  • generalisation - encoded info (translation, time-reversal, conservation) holds beyond the training distribution
  • sharp gradients and boundary conditions - Fourier modes for periodic flow, wavelets for shocks and edges, etc.
  • explainability and discoverability - confident the model is able to discover the relationships you're targeting
  • cost - FFT is \(O(N \log N)\), truncated graph kernel is \(O(N k)\), but Attention is \(O(N^2)\) in sequence length.

1D three-phase viscoelastic composite

A 1D unit cell of a composite made from three materials, subject to a cyclic strain. Each phase has its own Young's modulus \(E(x)\) and viscosity \(v(x)\), so both are piecewise-constant across the three phases. The constitutive law below couples the stress field \(\sigma(x, t)\) to the strain field \(\epsilon(x, t)\) and the rate of displacement \(\partial u/\partial t\). Goal: models have to learn the operator \(\epsilon(\cdot, t) \to \sigma(\cdot, t)\) rolled forward in time. \[\sigma(x, t) = E(x)\, \epsilon(x, t) + v(x)\, \partial u(x, t) / \partial t\]

Transformer. 4,209,205 params, purely data-driven
RNO. 52 params + PDE/time-series bias

4,209,205 versus 52 parameters, an 80,946x ratio . Same amount of data, same amount of tuning, both fit the data. The Recurrent Neural Operator wins by carrying two biases Attention has to learn:

  1. PDE bias - it's a neural operator!
  2. time-series bias - the same kernel/weights are reused every timestep, (Markov assumption). Attention has to otherwise learn this from the context/input sequence alone.

2D Darcy flow on the unit box

github ↗

Fluid pressing through a porous medium, 2D on the unit box. The permeability \(a(x)\) is a heterogeneous random field, forcing \(f(x)\) is constant. The Darcy PDE below gives the pressure field \(u(x)\) for any permeability sample. Goal: models have to learn the operator \(a \to u\). No iterative PDE solver at inference time, just a single forward pass! People think AI is unsustainable, but these neural operators are a potential replacement to the slow, iterative solvers running on today's HPCs! \[-\nabla \cdot (a(x)\, \nabla u(x)) = f(x), \; x \in (0, 1)^2\]

MLP + CNN. 5,982,121 params
wavKAN + CNN. 35,919 params + wavelet bias
MLP + FNO. 4,667,665 params + spectral bias

MLP + CNN at 6M is the bias-free baseline: locality only. MLP + FNO at 4.7M is the best fit. Its spectral prior on the IKO matches the elliptic PDE's smooth solution structure.

wavKAN + CNN at 35,919 parameters, 166x smaller than the baseline, gets close to FNO by parametrising the univariate activations as wavelets ( arXiv 2405.12832 ). Wavelets are local in both space and frequency, so they resolve features in permeability without spreading them across many modes. Slightly worse fit than FNO, much better params-per-fidelity ratio.

So is attention bad?

Not in the slightest! Attention is highly data-parallel and versatile. It's matmul-heavy and scales on existing hardware. The recurrent neural operator is sequential due to its recurrence relationship.

With abundant data and unknown structure, more compute is better than more research into bespoke architectures, especially when they're difficult to parallelise.

You want to be greedy with your hardware usage, not your time spent.

However, there's a difference between scaling a system and understanding one. I'd rather embed the secrets of natural language, the dynamics of protein folding, and the geometry of intelligence into a model than start from scratch and arrive at a worse understanding.

We can't always increase the amount of data we have, but we can almost always increase the amount of knowledge we have using data-driven means. This is moving up the y-axis in that first figure, commonly referred to as scientific discovery
